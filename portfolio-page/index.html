Portfolio piece One story: platform AI that had to learn the whole product

Braze · Lead PM · 2022–present

Operator wasn’t a feature. It was a literacy problem.

When BrazeAI Operator showed up as a real thing we might ship—not a slide, not a vibe—the uncomfortable truth was obvious: a “companion” that doesn’t understand email vs. SMS vs. journey orchestration isn’t helpful. It’s a party trick with enterprise pricing. So the work wasn’t only “define the assistant.” It was earn the right to sit across the whole platform by actually learning how each surface worked, where marketers get stuck, and what “take action” means when the UI is a mile wide.

Operator wasn’t “built inside the design system” in some tidy fictional sense—it was built by the design system team, tightly coupled with front-end infrastructure. That meant we could ship something real and still give teams a path to adopt the patterns on their own. The goal was the boring-good one: make the right choices the easy choices—shared conversational UX grammar, tokens and accessibility in the loop, fewer one-off chat experiences invented in isolation. Pattern first, then scale.

The part I’m proudest of is boring on purpose: making the weird, non-deterministic stuff feel legible inside a product culture that likes crisp edges. Shipping to GA wasn’t the end of that story—it was proof we could hold both: speed and coherence, customer value and internal reuse.

The tradeoff I still think about: depth vs. coverage. There’s always another edge case surface whispering your name. The win was choosing repeatable interaction grammar over bespoke brilliance—so teams could align faster than I could personally become the world’s leading expert on every corner of the app. (I tried the expert thing anyway. RIP a few weekends.)

Not a brand statement Off the clock (which is a funny phrase when you’re a parent)

Structured feelings Games & narrative systems

I design games where people sit around a table—or stand in a room—and play through a story together, live. No screens, no controllers: just rules, relationships, and whatever happens when a group of adults agrees to take the fiction seriously for a few hours. If you know me from work, think of this as the lab where I test instructions, safety, and “what happens when we mean it” before anyone’s OKR gets involved.

On itch.io: Pressure Drop, My Jam, Hack/Enhance, Foreboding, and Lorem Musarch (a cursed music font born from actual play needs—beautiful, wrong, useful).

I’ve also written, edited, or consulted on other designers’ projects—small-press RPGs, competition larps, and larger-scale productions like Inside Avernus. The range is wide but the throughline is the same: clear rules, honest playtesting, and collaborators who have opinions and deadlines.

The thing underneath all of it is safety. Not "safety" as a disclaimer at the top of the document—safety as a design principle. Players are more important than the game. Always. That conviction shows up in how I write rules, how I run retros, how I facilitate playtests, and (honestly) how I resolve conflicts when all three of my kids want to practice piano at the same time. It’s a weird problem to have, but it’s still a real one, and empathy-first is empathy-first regardless of the stakes. Once Kieron Gillen called one of my safety rules the greatest paragraph in a set of RPG rules ever, which is probably the only blurb I’ll ever need.

I also handled art direction for My Jam and Pressure Drop—I care a lot about how a game looks in your hands, not just how it reads. Both projects have incredible illustration work that I’m genuinely proud to have shaped.

Plot twist, but coherent Law, then games, then product

Law first: JD from the University of Chicago, then roughly a decade in federal IP litigation. Trial teams, messy facts, and a lot of translating experts for people who don’t live in the detail. What stuck wasn’t the courtroom stuff—it was the muscle memory: sit with ambiguity, explain hard things clearly, and don’t pretend the mess is clean just because the slide looks nice.

Games in parallel: I was writing and editing tabletop games the whole time. That’s where I learned how rules feel in someone’s mouth—not on a page, not on a slide, but live, out loud, with real people trying to follow them. Turns out "is this rule clear?" and "is this UI clear?" are the same question.

Product as the pivot: I left law because I wanted to build things, not argue about them. GovTech taught me stakeholders who answer to voters. Martech taught me scale. The through-line is the same one it’s always been: psychological safety first, then build from there.
